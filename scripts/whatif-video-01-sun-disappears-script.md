## PART 1: VOICE TEXT

**Copy ONLY the text between the two lines into the voice tool.** It has no headings or labels on purpose, so nothing extra gets read aloud.

---------------------------------------------------------------

At exactly twelve o'clock, the Sun disappears.

Milo is eating lunch on a park bench. Birds are singing. The sky is blue. And for the next eight minutes and twenty seconds, absolutely nothing will change. Not for Milo. Not for anyone on Earth.

Because the Sun is already gone. We just haven't been told yet.

Here's why. Sunlight takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds to travel from the Sun to us. So the light hitting Milo's face right now left the Sun before it vanished. It's like a letter from someone who's already gone. It's still in the mail.

And here's the strange part. The Sun's gravity is still in the mail too. For a long time, scientists argued about how fast gravity travels. Then in twenty seventeen, two dead stars collided a hundred and thirty million light years away. Telescopes caught the flash of light, and gravitational wave detectors caught the ripple in space, less than two seconds apart, after a journey of a hundred and thirty million years. Gravity moves at the speed of light.

So for eight minutes, Earth keeps going around a Sun that doesn't exist. Milo keeps eating his sandwich. His survival odds? One hundred percent. For now.

Remember the Moon. It's about to matter.

Twelve oh eight. Twenty seconds.

The sky goes out.

Not slowly, like a sunset. All at once, everywhere on the day side of the planet, like someone hit a switch. The blue sky turns black, because blue sky is just sunlight bouncing around in the air. No sunlight, no blue.

And in that same moment, Earth lets go. The pull that kept us circling for four and a half billion years is gone, and the planet flies off in a straight line, at about thirty kilometres every second. Milo feels nothing. No shaking, no wind. Everything around him is flying with him.

Then, about one second later, the Moon goes dark.

The Moon doesn't make its own light. It's a mirror for the Sun. Its last reflected sunlight takes just over a second to reach Earth, and then the mirror goes blank too.

Milo's odds just dropped to ninety percent. And he doesn't even know why yet.

But look up. The stars are out, at noon. More stars than Milo has ever seen, because there's no sunlight washing them away. And one of them is brighter than the rest.

That's Jupiter. It's still shining. Because the last sunlight that hit Jupiter is still on its way to us. For somewhere between half an hour and an hour, the last sunlight in the universe that we will ever see is bouncing back to us off a giant planet. Then Jupiter goes dark too.

Now it's just Milo, a black sky, and a clock.

Day one. The first thing Milo notices isn't the dark. It's the silence. Birds go quiet at night, and as far as their bodies can tell, it's night now. Forever.

Every plant on Earth stops making food at the same moment. Photosynthesis needs light. No light, no food. Most plants will be dead in a few weeks.

But here's a surprise. Big old trees won't die that fast. They grow slowly and store huge amounts of sugar, and some could hang on for years, maybe decades, slowly eating themselves.

Animals aren't so lucky. Plant eaters run out of food first. Then the animals that eat them.

Milo's odds: seventy percent.

Day seven. The heat that's left is leaking out into space, and there's nothing to replace it. Within the first week, the average temperature on the surface of the planet drops to around minus eighteen degrees Celsius. Colder than a home freezer. Everywhere. Including the places that have never seen snow.

Milo's odds: forty percent.

Month two. The top of the ocean is freezing. But this is where the planet does something clever. Ice floats, and ice is a blanket. A thick lid of ice traps the heat underneath, and the deep ocean stays liquid. Not for weeks. For a very, very long time.

And down there, at the bottom of the sea, there are cracks in the ocean floor called hydrothermal vents, pouring out heat from inside the Earth. Whole communities of tiny living things already live there without any sunlight at all. They won't even notice the Sun is gone.

Year one. On the surface, the average temperature reaches about minus seventy-three degrees. The air is so cold and still that it starts to feel like another planet. Because, in a way, it is. Earth is now a rogue planet, drifting through the dark between the stars.

So where is Milo?

If he's smart, he's gone where the Earth still has heat of its own. Places with volcanoes and hot springs, where heat rises from deep underground. With that heat, power, and food grown under lamps, a small group of people could survive. Not live well. Survive.

Milo's final survival odds: five percent. Unless he moved fast. In the first eight minutes, while the rest of the world was still eating lunch.

So let me ask you. If you knew the Sun had disappeared, and you had eight minutes before anyone else found out, where would you go?

Tell me in the comments. And next time, Milo falls into a black hole. He has one hour. See you there.
