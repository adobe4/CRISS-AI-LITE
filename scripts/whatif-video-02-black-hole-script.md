# What If video 02: "You Have 1 Hour Before Falling Into a Black Hole"

The channel is Milo's Odds. The episode was teased at the end of "The Sun Disappears".

## PART 1: Story idea (why this version is better)

The Sun video had no goal for Milo. Things happened *to* him. This episode gives him what every strong story has:

- **A clock:** sixty minutes until he hits the event horizon.
- **A goal:** get rescued before then.
- **Someone who loves him:** his sister **Nia**, a flight controller on the research station Halo. She is trying to save him.
- **A twist from real physics:** the rescuers can still *see* Milo hanging at the edge, and they think there's still time. But what they see is old, stretched light. He's already gone. This repeats the theme of the Sun video ("what you see is already the past"), so the two episodes feel like one series.
- **Odds that go up and down:** 12% → 61% (hope) → 0% (Nia has to turn the drone back, and Milo can't even answer her) → crossing the horizon makes it final → the last message (hope again).
- **An ending that lands on emotion,** not facts: the last message Milo sends home.

Runtime is about 1,260 words, roughly 8 minutes.

## PART 2: Voice text

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Milo has one hour before he falls into a black hole. And for the next fifty-nine minutes, he won't feel a thing.

Far in the future, Milo is a pilot on a little research ship, orbiting the black hole at the center of our galaxy. Four million times heavier than the Sun.

A few minutes ago, his engines fired to adjust his orbit. And they jammed. By the time they finally died, they had burned away almost all of his sideways speed. And around something this heavy, if you stop moving sideways, there's only one way left to go. Straight down.

No engines. No way to call for help. Just a receiver that can still hear the station, and an emergency beacon that blinks once every second.

He's a hundred and eighty million kilometers away. A little farther than the Earth is from the Sun. That sounds like a lot of room. It isn't.

The ship's computer does the math, and puts one number on the screen. Fifty-nine minutes, thirty seconds. That's how long until Milo falls through the edge of the black hole. The event horizon. The line that nothing, not even light, ever comes back from.

Milo's survival odds? Twelve percent.

Because he isn't alone out here.

Not far behind him is the research station Halo. And on Halo, watching his signal on a screen, is his sister, Nia.

She has one rescue drone. The fastest machine anyone has ever built. And it has exactly one chance.

Nia launches it.

Milo's odds just jumped to sixty-one percent.

Now here's the first thing that surprises everyone. Milo is falling toward the heaviest thing in our entire galaxy, and he feels... nothing. No pull, no pressure. He floats in his seat like an astronaut. Because when you fall freely, gravity is silent. Your body and your ship fall together.

And he's lucky in another way. If this were a small black hole, one just ten times heavier than the Sun, Milo would never make it to the edge alive. Long before he reached it, the pull on his feet would be so much stronger than the pull on his head that he'd be stretched thin, like spaghetti. Scientists literally call it spaghettification.

But a giant black hole is gentle at the edge. At the event horizon of this one, the stretch on Milo's body would be weaker than a fridge magnet.

The monsters are the small ones.

Minute twenty. The drone is closing in. But Milo is speeding up.

He's now moving at more than twenty thousand kilometers every second. And every minute, faster.

Minute thirty. Nia's voice comes through the receiver. She's been running the numbers again. And her voice has changed.

Catching up with Milo isn't the problem. The drone will reach him at minute forty-seven. The problem is what comes next. By then, Milo will be moving at a quarter of the speed of light. To save him, the drone would have to stop a whole ship moving that fast, turn it around, and drag it back out. Not even the drone can do that. Not even close.

And now Nia has to make the worst choice of her life. Keep the drone going, and lose it too. Or turn it back.

She turns it back.

Milo's odds just dropped to zero. And he can't even answer her.

Minute forty. And now the sky starts to break.

The black hole itself is invisible. It's a hole. A perfect circle of nothing. But around it, light from the stars behind is being bent. Warped around the black hole, like the world seen through the bottom of a glass. Stars that should be hidden appear twice. A whole ring of light wraps around the dark circle.

At minute fifty-nine, Milo flies through a place where gravity is so strong that light itself can go around in circles. Anyone who could stop and hover right there, and look straight to the side, could in theory see light that went all the way around the black hole. They could see the back of their own head.

And now, the strangest part of this whole story.

Nia is still watching. On her screen, Milo is slowing down.

His ship drifts closer and closer to the edge... but never quite reaches it. His light gets redder. Dimmer. His beacon, that blinked once every second, blinks slower... and slower... like a heartbeat running out. To Nia, it looks like her brother is frozen at the edge of the black hole, fading, but still there.

And part of her wants to believe it. He's right there. He's not even through yet.

But that's not what's really happening.

What Nia is seeing is old light. The last light Milo sent out before he crossed, stretched thinner and thinner as it climbs back out. Milo isn't frozen. For him, nothing slows down at all.

Minute fifty-nine and thirty seconds.

Milo crosses the event horizon.

And nothing happens. No wall. No flash. No bump. If he hadn't been watching the clock, he wouldn't know.

But everything has changed. Until now, at least in theory, a strong enough engine could still have pulled him out. Not anymore. Not with any engine. Not even light gets out from here.

Because inside a black hole, space and time swap places. The center isn't a place in front of him anymore. It's a moment in his future. As certain as tomorrow. Every direction he could turn, every path he could take, leads to the same moment.

And here is the cruelest twist. If his engines came back on right now, and he fired them as hard as he could to escape, it would only bring that moment faster. In here, fighting with everything you've got only makes the end come sooner.

Milo has about thirty seconds left.

You may have heard that right now, he'd look back and watch the entire future of the universe flash by. He doesn't. When he looks back, he sees the stars, bent and squeezed together. Somewhere among them, too small to see, is the station. And Nia. The universe is still out there. He just can't ever get back to it.

He never got to say goodbye. So he does the only thing left. He records one last message for Nia.

It will never leave the black hole. Nobody will ever hear it.

Then, in the final fraction of a second, the stretching he escaped at the edge finally catches up with him. And Milo is gone.

Or... is he?

Stephen Hawking predicted that black holes are not forever. Very, very slowly, they leak away. This one would take roughly a one followed by eighty-seven zeros, in years. Far longer than the stars will burn.

And some physicists think that when a black hole finally disappears, everything that ever fell in leaves a trace. Scrambled beyond recognition. But not erased. Not completely.

Which means, maybe, somewhere in the last light of a dying black hole, near the end of time, there's a tiny, unreadable piece of Milo's last message. Still on its way home.

Milo's final survival odds? Zero percent.

But his last message... might be the one thing a black hole can't keep forever.

So let me ask you. If you had one hour left, and nobody could save you, what would you say in your last message? And who would you send it to?

Tell me in the comments.

And next time, Milo wakes up, and every other person on Earth is gone.

See you there.

## PART 4: Facts and sources

All numbers were calculated for a free fall from rest toward Sagittarius A*, a non-rotating black hole of 4.3 million solar masses.

- **The black hole:** Sgr A* has about 4.3 million solar masses, at about 26,000 light-years (EHT 2022; GRAVITY collaboration). Its event horizon radius is about 12.7 million km.
- **Why he falls straight in:** at 1.2 AU from Sgr A*, a circular orbit moves at about 56,000 km/s (0.19c). Losing power alone would leave the ship orbiting, so the story uses a jammed burn that cancels almost all of its sideways speed. That leaves a nearly radial fall from near-rest, which the timings assume. Hovering was rejected because it would need about 1,760 g. A ship and drone with this much delta-v are clearly far-future tech, which is why the voice text says "far in the future".
- **Communication:** Milo has a receiver and a beacon but no transmitter. That's why he never says goodbye, why the recorded message matters, and why Nia sees his beacon slow down (gravitational and Doppler redshift of the blink rate).
- **Seeing the station:** a station about 170 million km away is about 28 magnitudes fainter than the ISS seen from the ground, so it's invisible to the eye. The script says "too small to see".
- **The fall:** a free fall from rest at 182 million km (1.2 AU, about 14.3 Schwarzschild radii) reaches the horizon in about 59.5 minutes of Milo's own time. He then has about 29 seconds inside.
- **Speed:** relative to static observers, he moves at about 0.08c at minute 20, 0.25c at minute 47 and 0.77c at minute 59. The script says "twenty thousand km per second" for minute 20, which is about 0.07c.
- **Tidal stretch:** across a 2 m body it's about 1/10,000 g at the Sgr A* horizon (less than a fridge magnet). For a 10-solar-mass black hole, it already reaches 100 g at about 1,750 km out, far outside its 30 km horizon.
- **Photon sphere:** at 1.5 times the Schwarzschild radius, which Milo reaches at about minute 59.1; light can orbit there. Seeing the back of your head applies to an observer hovering there, so the script says "anyone who could stop and hover".
- **The outside view:** distant observers see the infaller redshift, dim and appear to freeze at the horizon. In practice the image fades out within minutes for Sgr A* (the redshift grows exponentially on a timescale of about the light-crossing time). The infaller crosses in finite proper time.
- **Inside:** r becomes timelike, so the singularity lies in the future. Hard thrust outward shortens the remaining proper time (Lewis & Kwan 2007, "No Way Back"). A small, precisely tuned burn can add a few seconds, so the script only claims that firing "as hard as he could" makes it worse.
- **The lensing analogy:** the bottom of a glass is a lens, which is a fair picture of lensing. The old "water around a drain" analogy suggested swirling, which is wrong for light bending.
- **The "see the whole future of the universe" myth:** this is false for a Schwarzschild black hole (Hamilton, JILA visualizations).
- **Hawking evaporation:** t ≈ 2×10⁶⁷ yr × (M/M☉)³, which is about 1.6×10⁸⁷ years for Sgr A* ("roughly a one followed by eighty-seven zeros"). It ignores the cosmic background: the black hole can't start shrinking until the universe is colder than it is. The information paradox is unresolved, so the script says "some physicists think".
- **Rescue logic:** the drone has to cover about 87 million km in about 44 minutes (about 0.1c, so it's clearly future tech). Matching and reversing a ship at 0.25c deep in the well needs far more. That's why the script says catching up isn't the problem.
- **Story elements:** the station, the drone and Nia are fiction. The description should say "a science thought experiment".
